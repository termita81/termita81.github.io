    What does EV mean?

    A scene is bright - but how bright? EV (exposure value) gives us a shared brightness scale: a sunny day outdoors might be EV 15, and a brightly lit indoor scene EV 9.

    Each step is one stop: twice or half as much light. EV 15 is six stops brighter than EV 9 - that is 64 times as much light (2 to the power of 6).

    Many settings can suit the same scene. Match “Settings suit” to “Scene brightness” for a balanced exposure. Different combinations can keep the image equally bright while changing focus, motion blur and noise.

    How the settings number is calculated
    aperture stops2 + shutter stops5 − ISO stops0

    Count from f/1, 1 s and ISO 100. Closing the aperture one stop or halving the time adds 1; doubling ISO subtracts 1.

    Formula: log₂(f-number² ÷ time in seconds) − log₂(ISO ÷ 100). This app includes ISO so the result describes the scene brightness your settings suit; aperture/shutter EV alone does not include ISO. Shutter labels such as 1/30 s are rounded from exact full-stop values such as 1/32 s.

    Explore how a camera sees

    How bright is a scene, and how can your camera turn that light into a picture? Start with brightness, then explore the choices that make each picture different.

    A scene is bright - but how bright?

    We need a scale to describe brightness. A sunny day outdoors might be 15, while a brightly lit indoor scene might be 9. These numbers are exposure values (EV): a common reference for how much light a scene has, expressed at ISO 100.

    The numbers count steps, rather than equal amounts of light. Each step is one stop: EV 10 is twice as bright as EV 9, and EV 8 is half as bright. Outdoors at EV 15 is six stops brighter than indoors at EV 9 - 64 times as much light.

    Read more about exposure value on Wikipedia.

    One brightness, many settings

    A scene’s EV does not prescribe one set of settings. For an EV 9 scene, both combinations give the same image brightness:

    • f/4 · ISO 200 · 1/60 s
    • f/8 · ISO 400 · 1/30 s

    Going from f/4 to f/8 closes the aperture by two stops, letting in one-quarter as much light. Doubling the time recovers one stop; doubling ISO recovers the other. The changes balance out.

    These are the app’s rounded shutter labels; the exact full-stop times are 1/64 s and 1/32 s.

    Same brightness, different pictures

    Aperture
    A lower f-number, such as f/2, opens the aperture wider and admits more light. It also gives a shallower depth of field: only a narrow range of distances around your focus point looks sharp, while objects closer to the camera or farther away look more blurred. For example, focus on a person and their face can be sharp while the background is soft. A higher f-number, such as f/8, closes the aperture and admits less light, but makes a wider range of distances look sharp. The size of that range also depends on focal length and focus distance: a longer lens or focusing closer generally makes it narrower.
    Shutter time
    A longer time collects more light but allows moving subjects to blur. A shorter time freezes more movement.
    ISO
    ISO controls how brightly the camera records the light it receives. On a digital camera, raising ISO boosts the signal and its recorded brightness; it does not make the sensor collect more light. Doubling ISO, for example from 800 to 1600, adds one stop of brightness at the same aperture and shutter speed. You can instead use that extra stop to halve the shutter time and reduce motion blur while keeping the picture equally bright.
    Focal length
    A longer lens narrows the view and makes focus blur more pronounced at the same aperture and focus distance.

    In the example, f/8 gives more depth of field, the longer shutter time can blur movement, and higher ISO can make noise more visible. Choose the combination that gives you the picture you want.

    Read the exposure meter

    Scene brightness tells you how much light is available. Settings suit tells you the scene brightness your current aperture, shutter and ISO can handle. Match the two for a balanced exposure.

    If your settings suit a brighter scene than the one you have, the picture is too dark. If they suit a darker scene, the picture is too bright. The meter shows the difference in stops: +1 means twice the image brightness, and −1 means half.

    “Correct” means balanced for this simulator’s reference. You can deliberately make a picture brighter or darker.

    Choose who sets the exposure

    P · Program
    The camera chooses aperture and shutter time.
    A · Aperture priority
    You choose aperture; the camera chooses shutter time.
    S · Shutter priority
    You choose shutter time; the camera chooses aperture.
    M · Manual
    You choose both aperture and shutter time.

    You choose ISO in every mode. Automatic modes aim to balance exposure, but can reach the available settings’ limits. In M, compensate for a change yourself to keep brightness constant.

    ISO, noise and film grain in more detail

    Why noise appears: light arrives unevenly, and the sensor electronics add some noise too. Collecting less light with a faster shutter or smaller aperture makes that variation larger relative to the useful signal. Raising ISO makes the result bright enough to see, including its noise. This often looks like speckles, coloured patches or lost detail, especially in shadows. Raising ISO alone does not necessarily worsen the signal-to-noise ratio if the amount of captured light stays the same.

    A practical starting guide for modern digital cameras: ISO 100-400 is useful when there is plenty of light; 800-1600 is common indoors or when you need a faster shutter; 3200 is often very usable on recent APS-C and full-frame cameras; 6400 and above can still be useful when getting a sharp picture matters more than keeping noise low. These are starting points, not quality boundaries. Sensor size and generation, exposure, shadow brightening, noise reduction, cropping and print or display size all affect the result. A sharp ISO 3200 image can be more useful than a blurred ISO 400 image.

    See digital examples: Canon's EOS R7 and EOS 7D Mark II comparison includes ISO 3200 crops showing how detail differs between camera generations. Nikon's Z6III field photographs include a bird photograph at ISO 8000. These illustrate usable results, rather than a guarantee for every scene.

    On film: ISO describes the film stock's sensitivity, so changing the camera's ISO dial does not change the film already loaded. Faster films generally show more grain, a texture from the developed photographic emulsion rather than digital sensor noise. Shooting film at a higher exposure index and extending development is called pushing; this often increases contrast and makes grain more noticeable. At 3200, grain can be pronounced, especially on 35 mm film and in large enlargements, but its appearance depends on film, development, negative size and scanning.

    See film examples: Gregory Owain's Delta 3200 photographs and comparison with Tri-X 400 show visible but manageable grain on medium-format film. The ILFORD technical sheet explains that Delta 3200 has a measured ISO of 1000 and is designed for exposure at EI 3200 with extended development.

    In this simulator: noise is deliberately made easy to see. It illustrates the tradeoff between captured light and image quality, rather than predicting how noisy a particular camera will be at ISO 3200.

    Try three experiments

    Focus: start in Focus gallery. Use A (aperture priority): you choose the aperture to explore depth of field, while the camera adjusts shutter speed to keep brightness balanced. Focus on the 5 m target and compare f/2 with f/8. Watch which distance labels become sharp. Also try manual focus.

    Motion: use S (shutter priority) for movement scenes: you choose how long movement is recorded, while the camera adjusts aperture to keep brightness balanced. In Sunny beach at ISO 100, compare the jetski at 1/30 s and 1/500 s. Try the same comparison with the circling seagull in Golden hour beach, raising ISO if the camera reaches its aperture limit.

    Noise: choose Gray card and P. Compare ISO 100 with ISO 6400. The camera collects less light to keep brightness balanced; look for the extra grain.

    Look around and choose focus

    Click a scene thumbnail to select it. Thumbnails are fixed, balanced references; moving subjects are slightly blurred to suggest motion. The viewfinder uses your current settings.

    Drag the viewfinder to rotate the camera, or use arrow keys when it has focus. Use Reset view or Home to return. With “Drag to move room” checked, the room follows your drag: dragging up looks down and dragging right looks left. Unchecked, dragging up looks up and dragging right looks right.

    In AF, click to focus. The green square marks your point, and autofocus follows the depth at that point. In MF, choose a distance from 1 m to infinity. The gray card has no focus detail.

    Take, compare and keep photos

    Click Take photo or press Space. Open a photo from the bottom strip to see its settings; swipe left or right on the photo, or use Previous/Next, the thumbnails or arrow keys to browse. Turn on comparison to see two photos together. Escape closes the lightbox, and scene movement pauses while it is open.

    Photos are 1920 × 1280 JPEGs with EXIF camera settings. They last only for this page session: download what you want to keep before reloading. Deletion asks for confirmation.

    This is a learning simulator. Scene EV is fixed, exposure controls use whole stops, and the preview approximates depth of field, motion and noise. Real cameras also respond to metering choices, sensor characteristics and changing light.
